When the Safety Filter Fails: Exact Diagnosis of Infeasibility in Multi-Robot Control Barrier Functions

Chandan Kumar Sah1    Jishnu Keshavan1
1 Department of Mechanical Engineering, Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru

Sixteen underactuated robots. Every pair within sensing range is an enforced safety constraint, drawn as an edge coloured by the share θk,i of that constraint each endpoint is made responsible for. A single linear program chooses the division at every control step, and no local program is ever infeasible.

Abstract

Pointwise infeasibility of multi-robot control barrier function (CBF) safety filters offers little guidance on its underlying cause. We derive an exact conic certificate whose sign decides feasibility and whose value separates the problem into a demand term, controlled by the barrier encoding, and a supply term, controlled by the geometry of the input sets.

Three consequences follow. The supply is invariant to the choice of class-K functions, so retuning gains cannot create capability. There exist structurally degenerate states at which no increase in actuation restores feasibility. And the certificate's multiplier is sparse, naming the few interactions and agents responsible for a conflict.

Because the certificate prices any division of a shared constraint exactly, the optimal division is the solution of a single linear program, solved centrally and executed locally. Across 320 paired closed-loop runs it reduces infeasible control steps from about 50% for the uniform and capability-weighted heuristics to 6.0%, matching a centralized filter, and cuts runs containing a safety violation from 118/160 to 23/160, at 2.3 ms per step.

When the filter says no, what should you change?

A solver failure is a single bit. The certificate turns it into an answer to three separate questions, each with a different remedy.

1Retune the gains?

No. The input coefficients of the barrier chain are independent of every class-K function, so the supply term cannot be moved by tuning. Exactly one exception exists, through an off-chain penultimate gain.

2Add actuation?

Sometimes. We characterize structurally degenerate states at which every agent's thrust axis is annihilated by the constraint it carries; there, scaling the input sets by any factor leaves the certificate unchanged.

3Divide it differently?

Usually, and cheaply. The certificate prices any division exactly, so the best one is a linear program. It changes neither the constraint nor the fleet's capability, only whether the local programs can use it.

What the allocation does, and what it cannot

One conflict, three allocations, one state. Left: each robot's local margin. Three robots have no admissible input under the uniform split and two under the capability-weighted split; the linear program lifts the worst margin above zero, and five of eight robots end up at exactly that optimum — the signature of a max–min solution. Middle: the capability leverage ∂M/∂ρi is identical under all three allocations, because the joint certificate does not depend on the division. Right: what does change it — raising one robot's actuation moves the reserve at exactly the predicted rate, while a 50% increase for a robot of zero leverage leaves it untouched.

Which interaction is to blame?

The multiplier is sparse, and it names the conflict. In a ten-robot conflict with 24 enforced pairs, the dual support is a single pair, and only its two endpoints carry any leverage. Over 52 closed-loop conflicts, relaxing the pair the certificate names restores feasibility in 94% of cases, against 4–6% for the closest pair, the smallest barrier value, the smallest ψ, or chance. The blame does not grow with the fleet: the support averages 1.4 pairs from six robots to sixteen.

The same task, three ways

Identical initial conditions, goals and actuation bounds; only the division of each shared constraint differs. The uniform and capability-weighted splits leave some local program infeasible on 45% and 32% of steps and both fleets stall; the certificate-optimal split does so on 0.3% and keeps the worst local margin positive throughout.

Try the certificate yourself

What it does not do

The allocation targets feasibility, not liveness, and the two are in tension. A heuristic split that leaves a local program infeasible lets the robot relax its own share through a slack variable and move without honouring the constraint: it makes progress, and violates the safety distance in five times as many runs. Enforcing a constraint is necessarily more restrictive than enforcing it only when convenient, and the proposed allocation reaches fewer goals (28.9% against 43.4%) as a result. Deadlock is a known limitation of CBF quadratic-program filters generally; resolving it needs machinery beyond the certificate, which we regard as complementary rather than competing work.
